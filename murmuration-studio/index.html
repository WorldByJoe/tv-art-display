About the model

Each bird steers only by what it perceives. Every cue is a turning torque, so turns carry inertia. Each step, a bird:

  • matches the heading and speed of its 6 or 7 nearest neighbours, ignoring any in a blind sector behind it (Ballerini et al. 2008; Bialek et al. 2014);
  • keeps its personal space and closes on neighbours that drift off;
  • turns gently toward the edges of the flock as it sees it against the sky (Pearce et al. 2014);
  • is drawn back toward the roost and a preferred height, as in StarDisplay (Hildenbrandt et al. 2010).

At dusk each bird goes down at its own moment, sooner once two neighbours have. A settings file saved beside each video records every value used.

The model in brief

There is no leader and no director: 100,000 identical-rule agents, each steering only by what it perceives. A bird never jumps to a new heading; every cue is a turning torque, so turns carry inertia (Attanasi et al. 2014). Each simulation step (1/120 s), every flying bird:

  1. finds its 6 or 7 nearest neighbours, ignoring any in a blind sector behind its head;
  2. turns toward their mean heading, toward their centre when it drifts off, and away from any inside its personal space;
  3. moves its speed toward theirs;
  4. turns weakly toward the edges of the flock as it sees them, dark against the sky (Pearce et al. 2014);
  5. is drawn back toward the roost beyond a free radius, and toward a preferred height;
  6. adds a small private wobble, which is where flock-wide turns begin.

At dusk each bird goes down at its own moment, or sooner once two of its neighbours have, and homes on its own perch in a copse of seven trees.

What this study changed, and why

The first 100,000-bird runs shed stray clumps of tens of birds and split during sharp turns. Faster responses removed the clumps, and wider personal space restored realistic spacing.

Runs: 20,000 birds, 10 min of flight each, medians of 3 random evenings. A clump is 5–499 birds separated from the main body by more than 3 m; opacity is the share of a bird's view the flock blocks.

  • Why the clumps formed. Clumps were groups whose members' neighbours were all inside the clump, so they heard nothing from the flock. Across 88 earlier runs, clumping fell steeply as opacity rose (Spearman −0.88): see-through flocks give the projection rule too faint an edge to steer by. Doubling each bird's silhouette, with no bird moved, cut clumps on every evening tested.
  • Why faster responses. As first built, a bird took 0.18–0.27 s to answer a neighbour's turn; StarDisplay uses a 50 ms reaction time. At 0.05–0.08 s the clumps nearly vanished on all 6 evenings tested (12× and 16×). The projection rule scales with alignment, as in Pearce's model; holding it back at its old strength brought the clumps back.
  • Why wider personal space. Faster responses packed birds to 0.54 m, below the measured 0.7–1.5 m. Doubling personal space gave 0.81 m with no loss of cohesion.
  • Why steady environmental steering. Faster responses came with proportionally more turn damping, which also weakened the turns produced by the roost and height pulls, and the flock wandered out of view. Scaling those pulls by the same factor restores their effect; it changes no biology.
  • The camera now eases left, right or up when the flock drifts far from centre, and back when it returns; while the flock is near centre it does not move.

Departures from the literature: discoveries?

Not yet. A departure first means the model is missing something. It becomes a finding only when the model matches other independent data, the departure survives many evenings and several measures, and it predicts something measurable in real flocks. This study produced two departures and two testable predictions.

Published findingWhat the model didReading
Turns cross the flock at 20–40 m/s with negligible attenuation (Attanasi et al. 2014); manoeuvre waves outrun neighbour-by-neighbour reaction (Potts 1984)Turns spread at 2–5.5 m/s and the flock took about 4.5 s to come round, with or without faster responsesA gap. The model's big turns are flock-wide steering by the roost and height pulls, which every bird feels at once, not turns started by a few birds. It also lacks the anticipation Potts described.
Density does not depend on flock size (Ballerini et al. 2008b); real flocks are marginally opaque, about 0.3 (Pearce et al. 2014)With size-independent spacing, small flocks were see-through (opacity 0.05 at 2,000 birds) and fragmented under the projection rule; large ones held (0.33 at 100,000)An open tension. Pearce's flocks reach marginal opacity by adjusting density; real density stays constant. Either small flocks hold together mainly by neighbour rules, or opacity was measured differently. Worth testing.

Predictions that field data could test:

  1. Fast responses. Cohesion of large flocks in the model required each bird to respond to a neighbour within about 50–80 ms. At roughly 1 m spacing, Attanasi's 20–40 m/s waves imply 25–50 ms per bird, consistent with this; turning delays between adjacent birds in 3D-tracked flocks would test it directly.
  2. Opacity and splitting. If starlings steer partly by the flock's silhouette, see-through flocks (small or sparse) should split and shed groups more often than dense, large ones. Counting splits against flock size in field video would test it.

Not yet in the model: predators and the escape rolls that produce dark agitation waves (Hemelrijk et al. 2015); birds' visible roll as a cue; true starling-shaped silhouettes in the projection rule (built and tested, no consistent effect on cohesion at 20,000 birds).
