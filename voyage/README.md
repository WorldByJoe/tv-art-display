# The stars on the way

Every run picks a real planetary system and flies to it. It opens on our corner of the Milky Way with both ends of the trip marked in it, then takes the 4,737 stars known to carry planets and peels them away, farthest first, until only the handful actually on the way is left - the rungs of the ladder out from here in that particular direction.

## Running it

Open `index.html` in a browser. That is the whole of it - one file, no build,
no server, no keys. It is written for a 4K screen and scales down.

Pin a destination with <code>?host=</code>, using any host name in the archive with three or more known planets: TRAPPIST-1, KOI-351, Kepler-186, K2-219, Barnard's star.

Press `i` for the parameters it is running, and the arrows appear if you move
a pointer. A standalone copy starts itself again when a run ends; add
`?pin` to the URL to keep the current run going instead.

## What it is doing

- The corridor is a cylinder around the line from the Sun to the destination, opened until it holds nine planet-bearing stars. Measured across all 346 reachable systems that radius comes out between 6 and 33 light years, median 11, and it barely moves whether the crossing is 6 light years or 6,600. Watch a few runs and the spacing of the ladder in different directions is the thing you come away with.
- The order of a run: our part of the Galaxy, with three disk rings and the Sun's own orbit named; the sky peeling away; eighteen seconds circling what is left, to see the corridor in three dimensions; thirteen seconds at home with the eight planets; a dive past Jupiter; two minutes crossing, with the rungs going by; then the destination's planets one at a time.
- The data is real and baked in: a snapshot of the NASA Exoplanet Archive, with sky positions and distances for every host star and full parameters for every system with three or more known planets. The cone-shaped crowd in the deep field IS the discovery record - four years of Kepler staring at one patch of sky - and the farthest stars of all were found by looking straight into the galactic bulge. The destination is drawn from the 346 systems with three planets or more, with near ones favoured slightly so the line does not always vanish into the Kepler cone.
- Two of the three lies are cartographic. Radius is log-squeezed, so one picture can hold a neighbour four light years away and the centre of the Galaxy at 26,700. Across the corridor the scale is stretched six times while you look at it from outside, or eleven light years of width against six hundred of length would be a hairline; the panel says which is in force, and the stretch is off before the ship leaves. Behind the nine rungs a third tier of stars - anything within three corridor widths of the line - is drawn at a quarter brightness and left unnamed, so the corridor has depth without competing for attention.
- The third lie is the engine. The departure is real physics - a dive past Jupiter, which is how every probe that has left the Solar System left. The crossing is not: no drive we have covers hundreds of light years. The ship flies the constant-1g brachistochrone of hard science fiction, and the two clocks in the panel are worked out with actual special relativity - ship years from rapidity, Earth years from coordinate time. The fiction is the engine, never the arithmetic.
- The arrival is a gallery: seventeen seconds a planet, inner to outer, each with its measurements on a rail. The portraits are drawn from those measurements - radius, temperature, the light it receives - because almost no individual exoplanet has a picture, and a render driven by the numbers is more honest than a painting.
