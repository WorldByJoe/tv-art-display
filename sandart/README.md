# Sand Picture

A moving sand picture worked out from physics: four sands, water and a little air between two panes of glass 1.2 mm apart, just after the frame has been turned over. The sand rests on a row of air bubbles and drains through the gaps between them like an hourglass, falls in thin streams and builds layered mountains. Each picture starts from the last one turned over, and takes about an hour, close to a real frame's pace.

## Running it

Open `index.html` in a browser. That is the whole of it - one file, no build,
no server, no keys. It is written for a 4K screen and scales down.

There is no text on screen. Add `?hud` to the URL to see the picture's clock and how fast your computer is running it against real time: the physics needs most of one fast core to keep up, and a slower machine runs the picture slower rather than skipping. `?seed=` with any number repeats a picture.

## What it is doing

- What it was checked against: a 25x time-lapse of a real frame. Just after the flip the air gathers under the sand as one level row of small bubbles; 10-12 thin streams fall from gaps in that row; the top of the upper sand sinks in V funnels, leaving triangles standing on the bubbles; near the end the row unzips, the triangles fall as clouds and drape the mountains. The real frame took 78 minutes; the model's pictures take 48-77 (on a computer that can keep up).
- No grain is tracked. The picture is a grid of squares 1.2 mm across, each holding the share of its volume that is each sand, and the page draws one grain per pixel from those shares. The sands differ in grain size (0.10-0.25 mm), density and shape: a lone grain sinks through the water at 5 to 23 mm/s (Ferguson and Church's law, slowed by the glass), and crowded grains sink slower, so one stream sorts as it falls.
- The water is pushed by the weight of the sand in it and held back by the drag of the two panes, which damps any stirring in about a tenth of a second.
- The upper sand drains only as fast as water can seep into it to take its place (Darcy's law). Water seeps through fine sand far more slowly than through coarse, so a hole with fine sand over it passes about a seventh as much as one with coarse sand.
- Why a row of 3 mm bubbles can hold up a wall of sand: in a gap this thin, friction on the glass carries the sand's weight (the Janssen effect), so the pressure on the bubbles stops growing after about 3 mm of sand. A bubble rises out only through less than about 1.5 mm of sand. When the streams have dwindled the row unzips, one bubble every few seconds; that last rule copies what the real frame does rather than following from the physics.
- The mountains grow by avalanches, as a slowly fed heap does: sand landing on a face lodges up to about 4 degrees steeper than it rests (31-35 degrees for these sands), and once the steeper cap reaches the bottom the whole face slides and lays one layer from the toe up, fine grains under coarse. That is what makes the thin stripes parallel to the slopes (Makse, Cizeau and Stanley 1997).
- Where it still differs from the real frame: late in a real picture only 4-6 streams are left and three or four wide triangles stand on the bubbles, while the model keeps draining through nearly every hole and leaves more, smaller ridges; and its stripes are thinner, about 1.3 mm against about 2.3 mm in a photograph of a real one.
