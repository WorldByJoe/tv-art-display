# Six minutes of real lightning, pulse by pulse

Every dot is one pulse of one real lightning flash, recorded by the Geostationary Lightning Mapper on GOES from 35,786 km up. A flash is not an instant - it stutters through a median of seventeen separate pulses in about a third of a second - and each pulse is drawn at its own recorded moment and place, slowed ten times.

## Running it

Open `index.html` in a browser. That is the whole of it - one file, no build,
no server, no keys. It is written for a 4K screen and scales down.

Press `i` for the parameters it is running, and the arrows appear if you move
a pointer. A standalone copy starts itself again when a run ends; add
`?pin` to the URL to keep the current run going instead.

## What it is doing

- Size is the flash's optical energy, through a log, because it spans a thousandfold in a single storm. Colour is age: white at the instant it fired, cooling as it goes. The ring is the FOOTPRINT - the area of cloud top that actually glowed, at true scale on the map.
- The ring is not the size of the bolt. Cloud scatters the light sideways, so a channel a few kilometres long makes a whole thunderhead glow, a paper lantern lit from inside. The median flash here lit 455 square kilometres; the biggest lit 6,061.
- The satellite records a median of about 87 femtojoules per flash, in a one-nanometre slice of oxygen light at 777.4 nm. The flash that made it moved some 15 coulombs across a hundred million volts - of order a billion joules through a channel at 30,000 K, five times the surface of the Sun. Almost all of the difference is geometry: a square metre at the satellite intercepts one part in 1.6e16 of a sphere that wide.
